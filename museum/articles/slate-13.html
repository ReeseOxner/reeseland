“Who Does 11 Rounds of IVF? Me, Apparently.”

There’s a downside to how sophisticated IVF has become. For many, it takes far too long to see it.

Mia used to say she’d never do in vitro fertilization. It’s a detail that feels significant now, looking back on the three long years that she and her husband, Chris, have spent trying to conceive. “When we first started trying, I was like, ‘We can try for six months, and then I don’t want to try anymore, because I don’t want to have my first baby after I’m 35,’ ” said Mia. “And then, four months into it, I was like, ‘Hmm. Let’s try a little harder.’ ” Now Mia is 39, and she and Chris have done many, many rounds of IVF.

It’s hard to explain unless you’re in it. It’s not like you go into a fertility clinic imagining you’ll spend years of your life and most—or all—of your savings on various failed treatments, which can include trying medication and the less intense intrauterine insemination process before IVF. You assume whatever thing you are trying this time is going to work—or at least Mia and Chris did. And then each time something doesn’t work, something new seems like it might. “It’s like, take it one day at a time,” Chris said. “Maybe this first thing will work! OK, well, maybe the next one will work!”

They’d only intended to do one IVF cycle, and reluctantly on Mia’s part, at that. “I thought, Then we can say we tried it all,” Mia said. After all, she works in Massachusetts, where insurance companies are required to cover infertility health care. But when the first cycle failed, Mia’s medical team suggested a tweak to the medication protocol that sounded like an easy fix. Why not try again?

For many people who pursue IVF, they are entering uncharted territory, with no real sense of how long they might be there. There’s no obvious or easy answer about how many rounds to do, but one thing IVF patients will tell you: It’s rarely their medical team that suggests they stop trying. In 2022, Hakan Cakmak, a reproductive endocrinologist at the UCSF Center for Reproductive Health, published a paper in the medical journal Fertility and Sterility titled “When Is the Right Time to Stop Autologous Invitro Fertilization Treatment in Poor Responders?” A hard line for him, outlined in that paper, is if a woman seeking to get pregnant is older than 45, when the chance of a successful pregnancy using her own eggs is close to zero. But the broader question for everyone else? “This is the biggest challenge in our field,” he told me.

Fertility treatments are distinct from many other kinds of care in that they are not life-or-death. They are expensive: Though insurance coverage for IVF is expanding, it is in many cases still the norm that patients pay staggering expenses out of pocket, either because they have no fertility coverage through insurance or because the insurance has a spending cap on treatments. And the rate of success is lower than anyone wants, although it’s true that it has improved dramatically over the past few decades: Between 1995 and 2018, the IVF live birth rate per cycle in women younger than 35 increased from 25 to 52 percent. Still, that means the overall odds of success for a given person are still a coin flip, or worse. All this leaves patients like Mia and Chris to decide for themselves: How far are they willing to go for the possibility of having biological children, and at what point do they draw the line?

Avery and her husband live in Texas, and have racked up tens of thousands of dollars in debt in their attempt to have a child. They started the process during the pandemic, when student loan payments were paused. At first, they took the money they’d allocated from their paychecks for loans and put it toward IVF. “We were just throwing our money at our student loans and watching them get bigger and bigger,” she said. It was nice, for once, to put that money toward something for themselves. “That something turned out to be throwing money in a different fiery pit,” said Avery. (Avery is not her real name—where only first names appear throughout this story, they are pseudonyms to protect patient privacy.) They’ve now done six egg retrievals and eight embryo transfers, which have resulted in two chemical pregnancies (defined as a pregnancy loss before five weeks) and one miscarriage at 10 weeks.

Advertisement

Avery and so many patients I talked to expressed gratitude to have the option of IVF available even as they shared their painful stories of the process. With IVF under threat from Republican lawmakers, there’s an additional source of stress that there’s a limited amount of time the treatments will even be available. “We did three back-to-back because we had some fear that, in addition to my age, it’s like—is this even going to be available to us in a couple of years?” said Avery, who is 39.

After they resumed their student loan payments, they put $60,000 on a credit card to continue IVF. About 70 percent of IVF patients report accruing some amount of debt in the process. Estimates for the cost of the treatments range from $12,000 to $25,000 per cycle. More states are mandating insurance coverage for infertility treatment; according to the Kaiser Family Foundation, 21 states now require private insurance coverage of infertility services, and 16 states have a mandate to cover in Medicaid. But that still leaves more than half of all U.S. states without mandated insurance coverage for infertility, and cost is still a primary reason people choose to stop treatment. One 2015 survey found that about a third of female fertility patients reported stopping treatment because they could not afford it; another study found that women without insurance coverage were three times more likely to stop treatment after one cycle as compared to women with insurance.

Avery and her husband now have five embryos in storage. If none of those take, it’s over. They will not be able to take on paying for the process of egg retrieval once again. “It’s hard to make a decision like that based on finances,” Avery said. “I mean, it feels like that shouldn’t be a factor, right? We want to have a child. We want to love it and raise it. And we can’t.”

For other couples, cost is not the main factor. Diana Zucknick and her husband, Paul, have no limits on fertility coverage through Paul’s work, which has been something of a blessing and a curse. Previously, Paul was at a job with a lifetime cap of $40,000, and getting a job with better benefits was a big deal for them. It’s allowed them to do 11 IVF cycles … all of which have been unsuccessful.

There are so many reasons IVF can fail: Maybe it’s a matter of too few eggs, or poor sperm quality, or possibly uterine issues, to name just a few common obstacles. From the beginning— the Zucknicks had their first fertility consultation in March 2022—one of their main issues has been sperm deficiencies. To address it, their doctors have tried a number of tweaks, including a sperm sorting technique meant to improve sperm quality and motility. (They’ve paid out of pocket each time for that procedure; even “unlimited” coverage can still exclude certain treatments.) “If you would have told me years ago that I would end up doing 11 rounds of IVF, I would have told you, ‘That’s crazy. I’ll never do that. That’s crazy! Who does 11 rounds of IVF?’‍ ” she said. “Me, apparently.”

For their own sanity, they had to give themselves some kind of end point. They decided on three healthy embryos, having internalized something a doctor once said—that for every baby you want, you should plan for three embryo transfers. “I could not continue to do this hamster wheel forever,” Diana said. Now, they are waiting to see if that “three embryos” limit leads to a baby for them or not. They have successfully created three genetically normal embryos, plus three “mosaic” embryos, meaning a mix of normal and abnormal cells, and even just getting to that point has been an involved process. This past summer, they transferred two—one normal, one mosaic—but the transfer failed. Shortly after that, the couple put their fertility treatments on pause as Diana dealt with a number of health issues. They hope to proceed with another frozen embryo transfer later this year.

That there are so many things that doctors can try now has made the process feel somewhat limitless. Andrea Braverman, a health psychologist who has been working with infertility patients since the 1980s, says it’s one of the most significant changes she’s seen in all her years working with this patient population. “When I first started in the field, there were points where doctors would say, ‘We’re done. There’s nothing more I can do for you.’ That’s not the case anymore,” said Braverman, who was previously a chairperson for the Mental Health Professional Group of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine. “Now it’s: ‘You don’t have eggs? We can give you eggs. You don’t have sperm? We can give you sperm. You don’t have a uterus? We can give you a uterus.’ ” (Couples may also turn to surrogacy, which is a godsend, but can cost upward of $100,000.)

Advertisement

“You know, there is no end point,” she continued. “I think that’s the big difference over the last few decades—now it really falls on the individual to sort of say ‘Enough.’ ”

The problem is that the patient usually doesn’t have the expertise to make this call. Couples going through IVF run up against the uncertainty of medicine, the subjectivity that plays into deciding what protocol to try next, and the fact that IVF is a business. Cakmak, the reproductive endocrinologist at UCSF, says he often feels frustrated when his patients tell him about various treatments their doctors at private clinics suggested they try; too often, they seem to him to be unnecessary. (It’s hard to come by an estimate of exactly how much of the field is dominated by private clinics, but a 2022 study names fertility as the medical specialty with the greatest market share owned by private equity in the U.S.)

According to the ASRM ethics committee, practitioners should provide their patients with “clinic-specific success rates,” and should even go so far as to inform patients “if they are aware of other centers with better outcomes, where their prognoses may be improved”; they should also work with patients to decide together whether to continue infertility treatment. But many patients who have been through IVF report feeling more like it’s a “let’s just try one more thing” approach. “If there’s an incentive to do more, then the physician will do more, unfortunately,” Cakmak said.

When you’re trying to get pregnant, and it’s not going well, you start to see babies and pregnant women everywhere. Of course, it’s really just that your mind is primed to notice them more than usual, not that you’re actually surrounded by them. But Mia really does see babies and pregnant women everywhere, every day, because Mia is a midwife.

“My full-time job is delivering babies,” she said. “Going to work every day has really basically been torture for two years. Every day I go to work, and I am surrounded by people who get to have something I don’t have.”

It’s her second career; fresh out of college, she worked in recruitment at a nonprofit she describes as “like a religious AmeriCorps.” She was good at it, but she didn’t love it. “I was ready to make a switch, and I just kind of went through and thought, ‘What are the things I’m interested in? What are the things I’m passionate about?’ ” she said. “And I kept coming back to women’s rights and feminism and birth—and how amazing and powerful birth is, and how transformative it is, and how vulnerable it is. I just wanted to be involved in that.”

Switching careers is never easy, but Mia likes to work hard, and when she does, she usually gets what she wants. Her anthropology undergrad major was rendered basically useless, so she had to take all the science prerequisites, which she did while working full-time—and then the grad program itself was another two and a half years. “You’re in class or in clinical 40 hours a week, plus you’re studying,” she said. “It was very hard and very intense.”

Now, the career she fought so hard to achieve feels like a cruel cosmic joke. When the first round of IVF failed, she took it hard. She was shocked, really. “I went into it assuming it was going to work the first time,” she said. “People do IVF and they get a baby.”

“It’s like, why not?” said her husband, Chris. “Why wouldn’t it happen?”

All through their first round of IVF, and after the failed cycle, Mia was working. She describes her hospital as an “affluent” one, with an older patient population of pregnant people—around her age, in other words—many of whom got pregnant from IVF. “I take care of women who go to the same [fertility] doctor that I go to,” she said.

For a while, she held it together. But in April 2023, after she’d done one cycle that failed, something snapped. “I was admitting a patient and I started having a panic attack,” she said. It’s not like there was something about this particular patient that set her off, and it’s not like she was dealing with the drama of the delivery room. “I think the dam just finally broke,” she said. Her heart was racing and she couldn’t control her breathing. “Mostly, it was that I felt like I was going to break down into tears.”

She excused herself and stepped into an empty side room to collect herself. “It didn’t affect patient care—I wasn’t in a dangerous situation, or anything like that,” she said. “But it was just like, ‘I can’t be having this level of anxiety at my job. People’s lives are on the line.’ ” Soon after that, she requested a monthlong medical leave, with her primary care physician and her therapist handling the requisite paperwork.

For insured patients like Mia and Chris, the No.1 reason to discontinue treatment is psychological stress, according to a 2018 study. “People talk about IVF so casually, but it’s a huge emotional investment, and it takes a huge strain on the couple,” said Alice Domar, a health psychologist who has worked with infertility patients for more than 40 years.

She’s part researcher and part clinician, and co-authored that study on discontinuing treatment. If you spend any time in infertility online forums (and if you’ve spent any time dealing with infertility, chances are that you do), you may have heard a dubious-sounding stat thrown around that infertility patients have rates of anxiety and depression similar to those of cancer patients. Turns out it’s a real study, and Domar led it.

“I think the laypeople drastically underestimate the psychological impact of infertility,” she said.

What causes all those negative feelings? First, undergoing IVF is a demanding physical ordeal for someone hoping to get pregnant, with each step likely to cause uncomfortable, and often painful, physical side effects including bloating and cramping. On top of that, the hormone-releasing medications cause emotional side effects, too, like mood swings or libidinal changes. Not to mention the fact that the entire process really is a process, requiring the precise timing of injections and multiple trips to the fertility clinic per month (often necessitating time off work and the nixing of travel plans).

For some patients, the difficulties exceed the physical and the emotional, reaching into the existential. Olivia and her husband, William, had only done one egg retrieval when she decided the stress was too much. “It’s like we’re having these two simultaneous lives of having to live like we’re having a baby, and make sure we have a home and a space—but equally still trying to live our child-free life and pursue our careers and go out for drinks with friends and maintain those friendships,” she said. “We’re stuck between these two worlds.”

Uncertainty drives everyone crazy, to some degree. But Olivia really hates it, which is part of the reason she’s so good at her job, which is in risk assessment. “So everything I do at work is writing pros and cons of how bad each outcome might be,” she said. After one egg retrieval, she put her own life into that sort of assessment. “And I decided that I preferred either life—either having a baby or being child-free—over the purgatory of living between those lives.”

She made a decision: Two years or two egg retrievals, whichever came first. Knowing that there will be a definite outcome was better than the endless gray void of the in-between. “I felt like I was simultaneously getting the negative aspects of both lives,” she said, “while getting the benefits of neither.”

In July of 2023, Mia was pregnant. She found out at home, about as early as you can find out. At nine days post-ovulation—six days after her embryo transfer from her second egg retrieval—she took a test. Nothing. At 10 days, she took another test. Nothing. At 11 days, though, the second pink line she was waiting for appeared. Something compelled her to dig the 10-day test out of the trash, and, to her surprise, it was positive, too. “I don’t know if I just didn’t give it enough time or I just believed that it was going to be negative, so I didn’t recognize it,” she said.

She went to show them both to Chris. “I almost wasn’t expecting it, even though we specifically tried for that result,” he said. Mia had the presence of mind to ask Chris a favor. “I have a picture of my mom shortly after she found out she was pregnant with me,” she said. She wanted a shot just like it. “We took a picture at that moment, because I love that picture of my mom so much.” For Chris, in a way, the whole thing reminded him of what it felt like to meet his wife: like winning the lottery. “It was this kind of wild, pleasant surprise, almost in the way of coming across Mia on the dating app.”  

At the first ultrasound, eight weeks into the pregnancy, the doctor turned the screen toward them. Identical twins. No, she quickly corrected herself—identical triplets. In the weeks that followed, the couple told everyone they were close to, and put it on Instagram to tell everyone else.

And then, during the second ultrasound, at 10 weeks: Three amniotic sacs, one placenta—a rare occurrence linked to pregnancy complications. The placenta is thought to take over around nine weeks, and her doctor theorized that in her case, the single placenta wasn’t strong enough to support all three embryos. Indeed, the embryos had stopped growing at nine weeks. Mia was no longer pregnant.

From there, it seemed like the obvious move was to try again. Now, they knew she could get pregnant. In fact, if it hadn’t been triplets, her doctor speculated that she would still be pregnant. Let’s try again, they thought.

The third cycle, in January 2024, was a wash—she ovulated earlier than her medical team expected. An infuriating mistake, but again, one with a clear-cut reason to try again. The fourth cycle, in March, produced no blastocysts, meaning there were no viable embryos to transfer. Still, Mia and Chris wanted to keep going. Their doctor, however, did not.

“She said that after four cycles, it’s probably worth getting a second opinion,” Mia said. “It felt like they were giving up on me.” Cynically, she wondered if the dismissal was more about protecting the clinic’s success rates than anything else. It was brutal to hear.

But it was also, in a way, what she’d been waiting to hear from the beginning: an admission that this might not work. Why hadn’t they had this conversation a year and a half ago, when they started?

“I don’t think my doctors were honest with me about my chances,” she said. In her memory, they told her that their clinic’s statistics suggest most of their IVF patients will eventually have successful pregnancies. But that’s of limited meaning to an individual patient. “They don’t say, ‘Hey, you are 36 years old and overweight and have never been pregnant before, and you have failed all of these other things you’ve tried—so this is where you are at, this is your specific chance.’ They don’t say that to you.”

Clinics can’t, of course, predict the exact chances that an individual or couple will have a successful pregnancy, but the U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention does collect data from fertility clinics in the United States and calculates success rates from each reporting clinic. (A search function on the CDC’s website allows users to zero in on fertility clinics near them and see how nearby clinics’ success rates compare to the national average.) Overall, the Society for Assisted Reproductive Technology shows a success rate of live births per new patient (using the patient’s own eggs) at 64.9 percent for those younger than 35. For those 35 to 37, it’s 56.3 percent; for ages 38 to 40, it’s 40.8 percent. At 41 to 42, it’s 22.7 percent, and older than 42, it’s 7 percent.

Plenty of doctors at fertility clinics do try their best to give their patients a realistic picture of their own shot at success. “Success in fertility treatment is ultimately highly individualized,” said  Jonah Barods, co-medical director of CCRM Fertility of Miami, who said practitioners at his clinic account for important factors like age and ovarian reserve when consulting with a new patient. Carleigh Nesbit, a reproductive endocrinologist at Boston IVF, said she often pulls up the SART website with patients in order to calculate their odds of a live birth with more specificity than just relying on her clinic’s general numbers. “I also tell people, ‘These are statistics that come from a large group of people, but you’re an individual person,’ ” said Nesbit. “I have seen things happen before where I told the patient there’s only a 1 percent chance—and then it happens.”

It can be hard to look at the odds, however slim, and not see hope. Some wish there were more emphasis on the fact that despite how far assisted reproductive technology has come since its inception, the whole thing is still a gamble. “It’s almost, in my personal view, not meeting requirements of informed consent if you don’t tell them that there’s a significant possibility of not getting a child,” said Sofia Gameiro, a health psychologist at Cardiff University who studies infertility. She’s now developing programs for fertility clinics that would train medical staff on how to compassionately and effectively deliver the odds to patients. “It can actually make things easier when you get there, because you’ve managed your expectations.” She’s taken to calling herself an “angel of doom”: So many people in her field focus on the positive, on the number of patients who do achieve their goal of a healthy baby, while her research focuses on the people who don’t.

Mia thinks that if they’d even simply been presented with the idea that IVF is not a guarantee, they would have approached the process differently. She thinks they still would’ve done the first round. And probably the second. “But I think after that we would have reassessed sooner about what we want to get out of this,” she said.

Instead, by the time her initial doctor suggested a second opinion, they were already in too deep; for her and Chris, the idea of stopping now wasn’t even on the table. She’d been growing frustrated with her clinic, anyway, and sought out a new one that seemed like it would try more experimental, cutting-edge treatments. A new clinic, a new doctor, a pile of new treatments—and a new, bleaker attitude for Mia. She went into her first cycle assuming it would work. She went into her fifth assuming it would not.

Domar, the American health psychologist, disagrees with the idea that fertility doctors should set lower expectations for patients, even if those expectations are realistic. IVF can be a brutal process, she says—you need hope to get through it. Besides that, she’s skeptical patients would hear it, even if their doctors did try to level with them about their chances.

“I did publish a study, many years ago, where on the day of embryo transfer, we asked patients, ‘What do you think the chances are that you’re going to get pregnant?’ And the average number was 70 percent. In that patient population, the actual pregnancy rate was 35 percent,” she said. “So I think patients psychologically have to, in this case, double their chance in their own heads to motivate themselves to tolerate the treatment,” says Domar.

Maybe it’s hindsight, but looking back now, Charlotte, a 42-year-old IVF patient, does wish her clinic had prepared her for the worst. She didn’t realize how common miscarriages were until one happened to her, and she Googled it.

“No one prepares you for if it’s not going to work—so when it doesn’t, it’s just shocking,” she said. She miscarried at home at seven weeks, and was so flustered she flushed the fetal material, which meant they had nothing left to test to inform future treatment. And then she felt that she was left on her own by the clinic. “They should have an onsite therapist or a social worker, or somebody that can handle grief and loss.”

Mia was right about the fifth cycle.

In June, the retrieval yielded eight eggs, six of which fertilized, two of which developed into embryos, both of which turned out to be genetically abnormal. Nothing to transfer. There’d been nothing to transfer, in fact, since their second IVF cycle in the summer of 2023.

Mia looks after the couple’s finances, though she hadn’t exactly looked closely for a while. Even though they have insurance, it doesn’t cover everything, and there were copays. A bill would come in and she’d pay it, trying not to calculate how much they’d spent in total. “It’s not fun seeing that kind of money coming out all the time,” she said. When she finally took stock this summer, after the fifth failed cycle, she estimated they’d so far spent about $30,000 out of pocket. “That’s when I had the realization that we had used up all of our extra savings,” she said.

She and Chris thought they’d abruptly reached the end. “We went into the fifth one thinking we were going to do a sixth one,” she said. “And then it was kind of in the in-between that I realized, unless something big changes, I’m not able to do a sixth one.” Their insurance would cover the egg retrieval and any embryo transfers, but not the requisite medication, which for them would cost about $12,000. (It’s a higher cost than usual for the drugs because Mia was on a higher dosage than usual.) They did not at that time have $12,000.

They had started to try to resign themselves to a child-free life when they got an unexpected windfall—enough to cover the $12,000 they needed for a sixth try. The egg retrieval took place this fall, and just before Thanksgiving, they learned the results: Each of their seven fertilized eggs—the most they’d ever gotten—failed to develop. Their IVF story is over … probably.

There is, as Mia phrased it, still a “glimmer” of hope, though it’s faint. Remember her third cycle, when she ovulated earlier than her medical team expected? It’s possible that their insurance will allow for one more cycle to compensate for that error, though her doctor and the staffers at her clinic’s billing department have advised Mia and Chris that it’s a long shot.

In some ways, it’s a relief to know there is an end. They tried everything. One thing Mia and Chris agreed on is that they wouldn’t go into debt to pursue a child; they also are not interested in donor eggs or adoption, and can’t afford surrogacy.

Their story is a common one. What might it look like if our culture acknowledged that? Maybe it would lead to expanded insurance coverage, so those who deeply desire to be parents increase their likelihood of success. Maybe predictive technology would improve, so patients could get a better sense of their odds from the get-go. Or maybe there would be a change in our cultural conception of IVF—acknowledging that while there are many wonderful success stories of people having babies despite fertility challenges, it will not work for everyone. Maybe there could be a deeper acknowledgement of the reality that not all people who are childless wish to be that way—and yet, their lives can be full of joy and excitement, too.

Now, for Mia and Chris, there are two paths in front of them, one clearer than the other: If the insurance cooperates, and if she gets pregnant and if they have a baby, they’ll keep their life the way it’s currently arranged. They arranged it that way on purpose. They bought their house, a three-bedroom, with the assumption that they would be raising a child in it; Mia asked about maternity leave in the interview for her current job.

But that vision is starting to look fuzzy. What’s coming into focus now is a new, alternate path: A change of career for Mia, something that isn’t patient-facing. (She started sending out résumés almost immediately after learning of the failed cycle in November.) Moving back to Chicago, where Mia used to live. Buying a two-bedroom, two-bathroom converted warehouse condo in her old neighborhood. Going down to one car and mostly using public transit. Living the city life, just the two of them. “It’s just kind of this dream of a big change,” Mia said. “I just want a big, new thing.”

They expect to have their answer from the insurance company by the end of January. Either way, something will begin, and something will end.
