Dr. Gift

In 1995, a woman got a phone call that stunned her—and helped ignite an international scandal. It took almost 30 years to come full circle.

In the early 1980s, Renée Ballou and her husband had all the trappings of a perfect California life. Ballou was a sales manager for a carpet mill. Her husband owned his own business. They had a son, a happy little boy who loved soccer. But for Ballou, something was missing.

“I never wanted just to have one child,” she told me. “It was from the time I was very young that I had that in my mind.”

Ballou tried to get pregnant for a second time. After a couple of fruitless years, it became clear that she and her husband would need medical assistance to have another baby. Ballou started infertility treatments: dozens of trips to the doctor, daily injections, even surgery. She got her hopes up, only to be let down every time the procedures didn’t work. Finally, in 1987, she gave up.

• Video from Slate

Ballou’s life got harder in the years that followed. She and her husband divorced in 1992. Then her mother died, suddenly, from brain cancer. In the fall of 1995, she sold her house and was getting ready to move. Her phone was just about to be disconnected when she got a call she hadn’t been expecting. On the other line was a reporter from the Orange County Register.

“She asked me if I had any family there with me, that I probably needed to sit down,” Ballou remembered.

The journalist was calling about the fertility clinic where Ballou and her ex-husband had sought treatment almost a decade earlier. The FBI had obtained a list of patients whose eggs had been stolen by those doctors. Ballou’s name was on it. Someone had put her eggs into the body of a stranger, and that stranger had given birth to a son.

“I was in disbelief,” Ballou told me. “I was just, I think, in a fog.”

Ballou was shocked to learn that she had a genetic child living who knows where, being raised by people she’d never met. And she wasn’t alone. As she grappled with the unthinkable news, several other women were getting the very same phone call.

Dozens of hopeful patients had entrusted their dreams—and their genetic material—to a famous doctor who bent the rules to suit his own ambitions. Now they had suffered an extraordinarily intimate violation, victimized by a breach of medical ethics they never even knew to fear. That revelation would develop into the furthest-reaching scandal the fertility industry had ever seen.

For Renée Ballou, it was just the start of a harrowing personal horror story. It would take a landmark legal battle, 30 turbulent years, and the emergence of an unlikely friendship for her to get the ending she desperately wanted.

In the early 1980s, fertility medicine was still a new frontier. After the first “test-tube baby,” Louise Brown, was born in England in 1978, in vitro fertilization clinics started popping up across the world. But in those early days, success rates were low. “Nobody really knew how to do it,” Teri Ord told me. “Everybody was trying to learn.”

Back then, Ord was an embryologist. For her, those unknowns were the best part of the job. “You tried to make things better, get the pregnancy rate better, get the freezing better,” she said. By the mid-1980s, everything was getting better, and business was booming. Fertility medicine was a billion-dollar industry, and Ord worked with two of the most successful doctors in the field: Ricardo Asch and José Balmaceda.

Advertisement

Asch had moved to the U.S. from Argentina in the ’70s. Balmaceda had fled from Chile, under threat from the Pinochet regime. The doctors met at the University of Texas at San Antonio, and it was there that they had a major breakthrough—a procedure that made Asch a star in fertility medicine.

It was called GIFT, short for gamete intrafallopian transfer. A doctor would extract an egg from a patient’s ovary, then, via laparoscopic surgery, place that egg and some sperm in one of the patient’s fallopian tubes. When Asch developed GIFT, it looked like a step up from the standard infertility procedure, IVF, which at that point had a low rate of live births. Some patients also liked that GIFT seemed to mimic the traditional insemination process. That’s how Pope John Paul II felt, at least: The Vatican forbade almost every other assisted reproductive technology, but GIFT was approved.

Sergio Stone, a fertility doctor at the University of California, Irvine, was wowed by what Asch had accomplished. In 1986 he hired Asch and Balmaceda to start their own clinic at the school. “The university invested a lot in bringing not only Dr. Asch and Balmaceda, but also their Ph.D.s, their embryologists, their technologists—a big group,” Stone told me.

Stone practiced at the new clinic too, doing mostly uterine surgery and hormonal treatments. Renée Ballou came to see him to find out why she was having trouble conceiving a second child. After a year of less-invasive treatments didn’t work, Stone suggested that she try the GIFT procedure. “These doctors, they were going to be a miracle for me,” she said. “They were going to help me get pregnant.”

Ballou liked Ricardo Asch. A lot of people did: One former employee said he was so charismatic he could sell sand in the desert. He gave Ballou confidence that she could have the second child she’d always wanted.

Ballou began a treatment cycle under Asch’s supervision. She took hormone injections every day to get her ovaries to develop multiple eggs. She was supposed to come back to the clinic when her eggs were mature so Asch could harvest the eggs. He would then inject them, along with sperm, into her fallopian tube. But before the surgery, the doctor realized their timing was off: Ballou had already ovulated before they could capture the eggs. So she started the whole process again.

“The second time, I harvested, like, 24 eggs,” she said. “He was so happy that we had such a good amount to choose from.” Asch told her that he would take the four most viable ones and use them for the GIFT procedure.

At the beginning of Ballou’s treatment, she had been asked what she would want to do with any extra eggs the clinic might collect—the ones it didn’t use for her procedure. She’d been clear about her intentions: She did not want to donate those eggs. “I’m trying to get pregnant. I’m not going to give away anything—not my eggs,” she said. “Those are the ones I’m going to use again if I have to.”

When Ballou left the hospital after that second attempt, she had a feeling the GIFT treatment hadn’t worked. About two weeks later, the clinic confirmed it: She wasn’t pregnant. She considered trying again but ultimately decided against it. “I just said, ‘No, I just can’t do it anymore.’ We’d had to put our life on hold for so many years,” she told me. “That phase of our lives was over.”

In the decade after Ballou ended her treatments, the fertility industry exploded. The number of clinics in the U.S. grew tenfold, to about 300. Treatments were improving all the time, and thousands of Americans became pregnant through medical techniques that hadn’t been available to their parents. IVF success rates improved too, so the more-invasive GIFT procedure got much less popular.

But even as fertility medicine transformed American babymaking, very little regulation materialized to govern it. No one was pleading the case for more laws and more restrictions—not the clinics, which were doing a brisk business, and not the patients, who wanted children and were desperate for help.

In that environment, entrepreneurs like Ricardo Asch thrived. He owned two expensive homes in coastal California towns. He drove a red Ferrari with the vanity license plate DR GIFT, after the procedure he had pioneered. And he owned five racehorses, which workers at his clinics dubbed “the Alpo Express,” because they weren’t very good. Nevertheless, he told his staff that he wanted to win the Kentucky Derby.

But Asch wasn’t all empty glitz. Teri Ord, the embryologist who worked with Asch and Balmaceda at UC–Irvine, thought their sterling reputations were well earned. “They thought a little out of the box in a good way, to where they could improve things,” she said.

Still, after a few years working with Asch in California, Ord started to sense that something was off. Although she spent most of her time in the lab, not with patients, she heard stories. According to one, a woman had been on the operating table, under anesthesia, when Asch had left to take a call from his horse trainer. According to another, there was a lot of cash at the clinic, and some of it would go home with the doctors, who could potentially hide it from the university. And there was allegedly occasional stretching of the truth on insurance forms.

Advertisement

But one story Ord heard was far more disturbing than the rest. It had to do with patients who produced extra eggs. More than once, medical assistants told her, they’d been instructed to use donated eggs from women who the assistants believed would have objected to the donation. The assistants had spent weeks doing the patients’ blood tests, taking their vitals, and giving them injections, and the women had never mentioned wanting to donate their eggs.

“It was just weird,” Ord said. “And so people started talking in the clinic, but nobody had any proof.”

It was Ord’s job to make the embryos, and Asch was the one who told her which sperm and which eggs to use. “He was the one that handled the patients. He was the one that knew what they needed. He was the one that treated them,” she told me. She had never doubted Asch, but with all the rumors she was hearing, she didn’t know what to think.

Ord was afraid of what might happen if she accused Asch of something unethical, or even criminal, without any proof. So she didn’t say anything—not to the people in charge at the clinic, and not to the authorities. What she didn’t realize was that some of her colleagues were already speaking up.

In 1995 Kim Christensen was working as an investigative reporter at the Orange County Register when one of his colleagues, Susan Kelleher, got an astonishing tip. It came from an administrator at UC–Irvine, and the gist of it was: “Would you be interested to know that Dr. Asch has been stealing eggs?”

Christensen, who died in 2024, told me in a 2021 interview that he was initially skeptical. “My first thought was, It’s crazy that these doctors who were so well known would put their entire careers on the line,” he said. But then a staffer at Asch’s clinic gave the Register a stash of photocopied medical records. The documents showed that in 1991 Asch had harvested eggs from one patient and used them in another. That second woman had gotten pregnant and given birth.

The reporters wanted to find out if that first patient had given her consent—if it was a voluntary donation or theft. They found her address and drove to her house. “It was like driving with goose bumps,” Christensen told me. “It was quite a moment to have to tell somebody that maybe somebody else has a child who’s genetically yours.”

When they got to the door, the patient was there with her husband. The couple had a lawyer with them too—maybe because they had been tipped off by the source who had provided the documents. But they didn’t seem to know exactly what the reporters had to say. “The young couple both looked pretty freaked out,” Christensen said. The patient and her husband retreated to a bedroom and left their attorney with the journalists.

After showing the lawyer the documents they had brought, they asked the big question: Had the patient agreed to give away her eggs? The lawyer left to confer with the couple. A few minutes later, he returned alone, with an answer: “They’re devastated.”

The following day, the patient gave the reporters a statement. The egg transfer “was done without our consent or knowledge,” she said.

After the Register published its piece on May 19, 1995, the news got picked up by publications across the country. That first story proved that one woman’s eggs had been stolen, but there were further revelations to come. The Register discovered that UC–Irvine administrators had been aware of the allegations of egg stealing for at least a year. The university had already started its own investigation and had shut down the clinic a few weeks after the story broke. But UC–Irvine had yet to inform the suspected victims. That meant that in some cases, reporters like Christensen were the ones telling patients that their eggs had been taken.

Over the course of several months, the Register published a series of damning stories about the clinic. Patients filed malpractice suits, but the doctors refused to open their books.

The scandal kept expanding. There were dozens of victims, some of whom did interviews with Phil Donahue and Oprah Winfrey. For tabloids and talk shows, this was a dream story: famous doctors, gee-whiz technology, and parents discovering long-lost children. It was a narrative fit for a soap opera—or a Lifetime movie. The network had a feature film, For the Future: The Irvine Fertility Scandal, ready by the following year.

Renée Ballou first saw the news in People magazine, in a July 1995 story titled “A Fertility Nightmare.” Initially, she thought there was no way she could’ve been affected. If the doctors had done anything wrong, she assumed, it would’ve been years after she had had the GIFT procedure. But a few months later, Ballou got one of those phone calls from a Register reporter, who claimed that the doctors had stolen her eggs.

The journalist faxed Ballou a document that listed her as an egg donor to a woman who had given birth to a boy. Ballou did the math. If her eggs had been taken in 1987, that would mean the child was about 7 years old. She could barely wrap her head around what that meant: In a way, the second child she’d always wanted was out there, living his life—as a son to someone else.

Ricardo Asch never denied that some patients’ eggs had been misused. The evidence was irrefutable. He simply explained that it wasn’t his fault. “I’m very sorry,” he said on NBC News. “I didn’t know that was happening, and if I would have known, none of this would have happened.”

Asch’s lawyer said it hadn’t been the doctor’s job to pay attention to which eggs were going where. “Dr. Asch didn’t go in in the morning, unlock the door, clean up, do the paperwork, sign the forms, track the forms,” the attorney said. “He’s a surgeon. He harvests eggs. He does surgical procedures.”

In a sworn deposition, the doctor tried to pin it all on Teri Ord, the embryologist. “He said that I was the one that did the transfers and that did all this stuff and he just trusted me too much,” she told me. “I just started laughing because it was so stupid.”

Ord spent four days giving her own deposition. She explained that the clinic had two sides. There was the patient-facing part, where the doctors and nurses performed procedures and the staff collected consent forms. Then there was the lab, where the eggs were inspected and the embryos were made. Ord said that Asch was the only person who worked on both sides.

The Los Angeles Times reported that Ord gave “the most credible—and damaging—testimony” against Asch. He’d given the orders. He’d done the transfers. There were too many cases, and too much documentation, for it to have been an accident. And although his partner, José Balmaceda, had definitely been involved, the bulk of the responsibility lay with Asch.

Once it became clear that egg theft was a deliberate practice and pattern at the clinic, the case took on a more sinister cast. A power-hungry doctor had played fast and loose with human life. At least 15 children were born from stolen eggs. Some analysts in the media suggested that the fertility industry had gotten too big too fast, before anyone had settled on the right and wrong of it all. But this branch of medicine was so new that no one was sure what laws, if any, the doctors might have broken.

They could be charged with mail fraud, because they had done some shady stuff with insurance billing. They had also prescribed a drug that was used in South America but wasn’t approved by the U.S. Food and Drug Administration. But stealing genetic material from dozens of women and creating babies without their permission? In the mid-1990s, there wasn’t a law against any of that.

Still, no matter what the law said, taking eggs without a patient’s consent was clearly a violation. The doctors, it seemed, didn’t want to take their chances with a judge or jury. In the summer of 1995, around the time the FBI started investigating the fertility clinic at UC–Irvine, Asch and Balmaceda left the country. Balmaceda went back to Chile; Asch fled to Mexico. A decade earlier, they’d revolutionized the American fertility industry. Now they were on the run.

At this point, only one of the clinic’s doctors was still in the United States: Sergio Stone.

“After they left, I thought that I was in deep trouble,” Stone told me. “But I was naive enough to believe that when the facts were out, I was going to be spared an injustice.”

In the months after the other doctors skipped town, Stone became severely depressed. He couldn’t stop thinking about the harm that had been done to his patients, especially the ones who didn’t get pregnant while other women gave birth to their genetic children.

A few months after Stone learned what the other doctors had done, he was arrested. A jury ultimately found him guilty of insurance fraud, for which he paid a fine and served a year of house arrest. After his probation, he kept his medical license, but he hasn’t practiced in decades. “I simply could not. My depression, my nervousness—I became very scared of making a mistake again,” he said. “It completely destroyed my life.”

Renée Ballou felt the same. She was furious with Ricardo Asch for what he’d done to her, especially considering that he had seemingly gotten away with it. But the bulk of her feelings centered on the boy who had been born from her egg. She wondered what he might look like, if he was healthy, and what kind of family was raising him.

The reporter who’d contacted Ballou was also in touch with the child’s birth parents, and she offered to make a connection. Ballou met them over speakerphone. “They were very defensive,” she told me. “They were protecting their son. They were just being very standoffish, not warm, just, you know, Who are you … and why are we having to have this conversation?”

Ballou asked the birth mother, Carole LieberWilkins, what the doctors had told her about the egg she had gotten. “The only thing she knew was … blond hair, blue-eyed, and that they considered those to be the ‘Gucci eggs’ ”—the highest-quality, most desirable genetic material available.

The two women agreed to do a DNA test. The results were definitive: The boy, whose name was Daniel Wilkins, was Ballou’s genetic son.

Every child born from a stolen egg raised the same set of uncomfortable questions. Some were personal: What role should the woman whose eggs were stolen play in the life of the child who had her genes? There were legal questions too. This wasn’t like adoption, or surrogacy, or voluntary egg donation. The genetic mothers had never signed away their parental rights. One victim, whose eggs became another family’s twins, sued for custody of the children, who were 6 years old by then.

When Ballou found out she had a second genetic child, she wanted to know what her options were. Her attorney urged her to pursue visitation rights, but she decided against it. “If he would have been 1 or 2, I might have gone through for visitation, but not at 7 or 8,” she said. “That would have really, I think, been horrible for him.”

But Ballou kept in touch with LieberWilkins and her husband. For a while, she had to go through a lawyer every time she wanted to talk, because the couple wouldn’t share their phone number or address. It took them years to even reveal their last name. Ballou couldn’t blame them for being scared. One couple who’d had their eggs stolen admitted to finding the birth parents’ address and having their genetic children videotaped.

LieberWilkins “asked me one time, ‘Can you believe that some of these parents are trying to take pictures of these kids?’ ” Ballou told me. “And I said, ‘Yes, I can believe that, because if I knew where you lived, I’d be taking a picture too.’ ”

The aftereffects of the violation Ballou had suffered took over her life. She spent a lot of time trying to find a way to hold UC–Irvine accountable. The school’s administrators said they had had no way of knowing what the doctors were doing in the clinic, and that they had shut it down as soon as they found out. But the school settled with about 140 victims rather than fight them in court, for a total of more than $27 million.

A lot of those settlements went to women whose eggs had been stolen but didn’t result in live births. Victims like Ballou, who had genetic children born to someone else, got the biggest payouts. Ballou received $460,000—about $930,000 in today’s money. But the sum didn’t provide her much comfort. She told me she started drinking heavily, “just to stuff the pain down and to make it through.” It took her several years to get sober.

Ballou struggled constantly to deal with her complex emotions. She felt grateful to be in touch with LieberWilkins and to learn more about Daniel, but it was agonizing to have a tiny window into the life of the child she never knew.

When Daniel was about 8 years old, LieberWilkins offered to put him on the line when she was on the phone with Ballou. LieberWilkins told him that Ballou was a friend from work. “I felt so thankful to her that she trusted me enough to let me speak with him,” Ballou said. “I thought, Oh my gosh, I can’t blow this.”

Ballou asked Daniel about soccer and school. She got the impression that he was a very happy child, doing the kinds of things her son had done at the same age. It was a relief to sense that he was OK.

But Ballou still had questions about the doctors at the UC–Irvine clinic—ones that continue to haunt her.

“I would really want to know why they did it,” she told me. “I’ve kept my head above water and I’m a strong woman, but it pretty much has destroyed my life.”

Why would these doctors risk their reputations, and their life’s work, to do something that was so clearly unethical? A lot of people speculated that it was about money. More successful pregnancies meant more cash, more fancy houses, more cars, more racehorses. But Ballou wants to believe that Asch thought he was doing a good deed. Maybe he didn’t want viable eggs to go to waste when he had other patients who needed them.

That’s what Asch told Mary Dodge and Gilbert Geis, the authors of Stealing Dreams: A Fertility Clinic Scandal, when they interviewed him in 1999. Hypothetically, he said, “if I would have done any of those things they tell me I did, it was to make someone happy. To help a baby be born. To have a family. This was the motive.”

In the years after Asch left the country, the U.S. tried and failed to extradite him from Mexico and Argentina on counts of tax evasion and mail fraud. He continued his practice in Mexico City and opened offices in Cancún and Acapulco. He still works with a Mexico City fertility clinic to this day. When I reached out to him for comment recently, he told me, “I don’t talk to reporters about all the UCI issues,” and directed me to a website he maintains in his defense.

Balmaceda settled back in Chile, working at a prestigious fertility practice. Two of his kids stayed in the U.S. One is the actor Pedro Pascal. Balmaceda was able to move back to the U.S. after pleading guilty to tax fraud in 2022. Now his son occasionally brings him to red-carpet events.

After the Irvine egg-stealing scandal broke in 1995, the main response across the country was outrage—that the doctors had been allowed to make babies with whatever genetic material they pleased, and that Asch and Balmaceda had escaped without any punishment. California did make stealing human eggs a felony in 1996. But for all the indignation that followed the Irvine crisis, there was no large-scale push for reform. Three decades later, such a movement still hasn’t materialized. Every now and then, a state legislature will respond to some outrageous abuse, like when Colorado made it a crime for a doctor to inseminate patients with his own sperm without consent. But all these years later, the laws that govern fertility medicine really haven’t changed much. That’s partly because violations like the ones committed in Irvine seem as if they must be illegal already, even when they aren’t.

It’s also impossible to write legislation about eggs and embryos without weighing in on when life begins—a question with enormous legal implications. If an embryo is property, stealing one is theft. If it’s a person, taking one without consent is kidnapping. In the current political landscape, with laws on reproductive medicine varying widely from state to state, legislators beholden to competing interest groups—IVF patients who view their embryos as children, well-heeled fertility doctors wary of taking on excessive liability, activists on both sides of the abortion debate—can be loath to touch the issue.

Instead, abuses and slipups in fertility medicine are typically left to the courts, which occasionally hand down decisions that upend the industry. After the Alabama Supreme Court ruled in 2024 that the accidental destruction of three couples’ embryos constituted the “wrongful death” of “unborn children,” fertility clinics in the state immediately stopped providing IVF services. The Legislature quickly passed a law shielding patients and doctors from criminal and civil charges for any embryos that perish in the standard course of treatment. But the law was just a stopgap measure: The ruling defining embryos as unborn children still stands, raising countless unanswered questions about their proper stewardship.

Ballou and LieberWilkins met in person for the first time in 2010, almost 15 years after they initially spoke. Both women were nervous, but the conversation was easy and warm. Ballou wanted to know everything about Daniel, who by this point was a young man. LieberWilkins had questions too, about Ballou’s son—his disposition, his educational history—believing that his life story could give her clues about her own child.

The two women started meeting up every couple of years. They developed a singular friendship, brought together by an experience no one else could understand. Still, they sometimes talked past each other. Once, LieberWilkins brought Daniel’s baby pictures to show Ballou over lunch. Looking at the photos, “I just started bawling,” Ballou said.

LieberWilkins was confused and asked what was the matter. “I said, ‘Because I’m thinking of the time I could have had with him,’ ” Ballou recalled. Although she considers herself a kind of mother to Daniel, she has never pressed LieberWilkins to use that term. “We just kind of stay off that subject,” Ballou said.

When Daniel was a teenager, Ballou asked LieberWilkins if she could meet him. Daniel relayed the message that he wasn’t interested. Ballou asked again every year or so. She hoped that maybe once he turned 21, finished college, or reached some other life milestone, he’d change his mind. They were both living in Southern California; it would be easy enough. But over the next two decades, he never wavered. Ballou followed his life from afar, through the stories and the photos LieberWilkins shared. When I first spoke with Ballou five years ago, she told me that she didn’t know if she’d ever get to meet her biological son. “The hardest thing is living with the fact that he’s out there, I know where he is, and he doesn’t want to meet me,” she said. “That’s very, very painful.”

Then, in 2024, something shifted. LieberWilkins had been open with Daniel about Ballou and his conception since he was a teen. In a 2024 interview with the Guardian, he said he had never been too curious about his origins. But once he got married, his wife wanted to know more. She searched the internet for information about Asch, found an interview with Ballou, and urged Daniel to get in touch. He started texting with Ballou, and they agreed to meet on Zoom.

Logging on to the call, “I was so nervous and excited,” Ballou told me. “What if he just wanted to say, ‘I don’t want you in my life’?”

That wasn’t how it played out. The two of them spent an hour getting to know each other: their interests, their career paths, their health histories. They had both brought a pad and pen in case they wanted to take notes, a similarity they laughed about. Daniel was impressed by Ballou’s outdoorsy hobbies, like Jet Skiing, and her close friend group of the past 50 years—four couples who call themselves the “Core Four.” Ballou already knew a lot about Daniel’s life through his mother, but she wanted to hear it all again, from him.

A few months later, they decided to meet in person at a steakhouse in Long Beach, along with their spouses and Ballou’s son and daughter-in-law. Ballou spotted Daniel in the parking lot when they arrived. “I’m a hugger. So I just ran up and hugged him,” she said. She was elated when the boy she’d been imagining for 30 years hugged back. For Ballou, the evening they spent talking and laughing around the dinner table felt like a dream. It was the first of multiple family gatherings that she once doubted would ever happen.

After that first meal together, Ballou posed for a photo in front of a Christmas tree with the men she thinks of as her two sons, who happened to be wearing nearly identical outfits. As they all smiled for the picture, Ballou was overcome by a feeling of gratitude for LieberWilkins and Daniel, laced with disbelief. So many other victims of the UC–Irvine clinic never got the closure she’d longed for. “You know, it could have gone either way,” Ballou said. “I always knew he was well taken care of. I knew what his life was like. But to have him in my life—I just feel so complete.”
